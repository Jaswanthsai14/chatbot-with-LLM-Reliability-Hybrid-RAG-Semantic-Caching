# Indian History: A Comprehensive Reference for RAG Chatbots

> **Scope and method:** This reference surveys the history of the Indian subcontinent, with emphasis on regions that form the Republic of India today while acknowledging connected histories across present-day Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, and wider Asia. Dates before the Common Era (BCE) are approximate where evidence is uncertain. Historical interpretations evolve as archaeological, epigraphic, literary, and scientific evidence changes. Traditional accounts are identified as traditions rather than treated automatically as verified chronology.

## 1. Prehistoric India

### Overview

**Period:** From the earliest known hominin presence, more than a million years ago in some interpretations of the archaeological record, to the emergence of writing and early states in different regions during the third and second millennia BCE. The dates and boundaries vary by region.

**Location:** The subcontinent, including the Siwalik region, the Indus and Ganges plains, central India, the Deccan, and southern India.

**Background:** Prehistory is studied mainly through stone tools, animal bones, plant remains, burials, rock art, settlement remains, and scientific dating. Because written records are absent or not yet deciphered, interpretations are provisional.

### Stone Age periods

- **Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age):** Characterized by stone tools and mobile foraging communities. Lower Palaeolithic tools include handaxes and cleavers in several regions; Middle and Upper Palaeolithic traditions show changing tool forms and, in later periods, more specialized technologies.
- **Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age):** Often associated with microliths—small stone tools used independently or fitted into composite tools. Communities continued hunting and gathering, with varied degrees of seasonal mobility and local adaptation.
- **Neolithic (New Stone Age):** In different regions, communities increasingly cultivated plants, managed animals, made ground and polished stone tools, and established more settled villages. These changes occurred at different times and did not follow one uniform path.
- **Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone Age):** In many regions, copper objects appeared alongside stone tools. Farming villages, pottery traditions, craft production, and regional exchange developed in varied forms.
- **Megalithic traditions:** In parts of peninsular India, especially during the first millennium BCE and later, communities built or used large-stone burial and commemorative structures. “Megalithic” describes a broad archaeological category, not one single culture.

### Important prehistoric sites

- **Attirampakkam, Tamil Nadu:** A major Lower Palaeolithic site. Research has reported very early Acheulean and Middle Palaeolithic evidence, with dates depending on the archaeological layer and method.
- **Bhimbetka rock shelters, Madhya Pradesh:** Preserve long sequences of human occupation and rock paintings from different periods. The paintings depict animals, people, and activities; not every image can be dated precisely.
- **Hunsgi and Isampur, Karnataka:** Important Lower Palaeolithic localities associated with stone-tool production and prehistoric landscapes.
- **Narmada Valley, central India:** Has yielded important hominin fossil and stone-tool evidence. The taxonomic identity and dating of some finds remain subjects of scholarly discussion.
- **Belan Valley, Uttar Pradesh:** Contains evidence from prehistoric and early farming contexts.
- **Bagor, Rajasthan:** A significant Mesolithic site with evidence of hunter-gatherer occupation and animal use.
- **Langhnaj, Gujarat:** Known for Mesolithic remains, including human burials and microlithic tools.
- **Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan:** A key early farming settlement in the northwestern subcontinent, with evidence of cultivation, animal husbandry, craft activity, and changing settlement life from the seventh millennium BCE onward.
- **Burzahom, Kashmir:** Known for Neolithic pit dwellings, bone and stone tools, and burials, including burials associated with animals.
- **Chirand, Bihar:** A Neolithic settlement known for bone tools and evidence of early village life.
- **Hallur and other southern Indian sites:** Contribute evidence for regional Neolithic economies, including cattle keeping and millet cultivation.

### Neolithic settlements and the transition to farming

**Period:** Dates vary regionally; early farming at Mehrgarh begins in the seventh millennium BCE, while other Neolithic traditions developed later in Kashmir, the Ganges region, and the southern peninsula.

**Background and developments:** The adoption of cultivation and animal management was not a single invention spreading uniformly across India. Communities experimented with local crops and animals, and hunting, gathering, herding, and farming often coexisted.

**Significance:** Prehistoric evidence demonstrates long-term human adaptation, technological innovation, and regional diversity. It also cautions against treating “civilization” as a simple progression from hunting to farming to cities.

**Related topics:** Mehrgarh; Indus Valley Civilization; Neolithic Kashmir; southern Indian Neolithic traditions; megalithic cultures.

## 2. Indus Valley Civilization

### Overview and chronology

**Period:** Early Harappan phases broadly c. 3300–2600 BCE; Mature Harappan urban phase c. 2600–1900 BCE; Late Harappan regional phases after c. 1900 BCE. Chronologies vary by site.

**Location:** The Indus basin and adjoining regions, including parts of present-day Pakistan and northwestern and western India. Major settlements extended into Gujarat, Haryana, Rajasthan, and Punjab.

**Background:** The Indus Valley Civilization, also called the Harappan Civilization, was one of the major Bronze Age urban civilizations. “Harappan” is often preferred because sites extend well beyond the Indus Valley itself.

### Harappa and Mohenjo-daro

- **Harappa:** Located in Punjab, Pakistan. It is one of the type-sites of the civilization and gives the culture its common name. Excavations revealed planned settlement areas, craft activity, burials, and material culture shared with other Harappan sites.
- **Mohenjo-daro:** Located in Sindh, Pakistan. It is notable for planned streets, brick architecture, wells, drains, and the structure popularly called the Great Bath. The precise function of some buildings remains uncertain.
- **Other important sites:** Dholavira in Gujarat, Rakhigarhi in Haryana, Kalibangan in Rajasthan, Lothal in Gujarat, Banawali in Haryana, and Ganweriwala in Pakistan. Their size, layout, water management, craft production, and regional settings vary.

### Urban planning and infrastructure

Harappan settlements often used standardized fired and mud bricks, planned streets, wells, drains, and designated craft or residential areas. Many houses had access to water and drainage, though infrastructure differed from city to city. Dholavira is especially known for reservoirs and water-management structures. The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro may have had a ritual or communal function, but its exact use is not proven.

Urban order suggests organized labor and shared conventions. It does not by itself prove that every city had the same political system or a centralized empire.

### Economy, agriculture, and trade

Harappan economies combined agriculture, animal husbandry, craft production, and exchange. Wheat and barley were important in several areas; millet, pulses, sesame, and other crops varied regionally. Cattle, sheep, goats, and other animals were used. Artisans worked with shell, stone, copper, bronze, faience, beads, ceramics, and textiles, although organic materials survive poorly.

Harappan weights and measures show standardization. Seals and other objects connect Harappan communities with regions of Afghanistan, Iran, the Persian Gulf, and Mesopotamia. Mesopotamian texts refer to a place called **Meluhha**, commonly associated by scholars with the Indus region, although details of that identification and the routes involved remain debated. Lothal has often been interpreted as a port-related site, but the precise function of its basin-like structure is disputed.

### Art, seals, script, and religion

- **Seals:** Usually small, often made of steatite, and commonly engraved with animals and short inscriptions. The “unicorn” motif is frequent. Seals may have served administrative, commercial, or identity-related purposes, but their exact uses varied or remain uncertain.
- **Sculpture and craft:** Finds include the bronze figure popularly called the “Dancing Girl,” a bearded stone figure often called the “Priest-King” (both modern labels, not proven identities), terracotta figurines, beads, pottery, and finely made ornaments.
- **Script:** Harappan inscriptions are generally short and remain undeciphered. No widely accepted decipherment exists, so claims about the language, rulers, or religious doctrines must be treated cautiously.
- **Religious practices:** Figurines, animal motifs, unusual structures, and possible ritual installations have prompted many interpretations. It is not possible to confidently identify a single Harappan religion or directly equate particular objects with later Hindu deities. Ritual practices may have been diverse.

### Decline and transformation

**Period:** Major urban contraction began around 1900 BCE, followed by varied Late Harappan regional cultures.

**Evidence and interpretations:** Many large cities lost population or changed in layout; long-distance exchange and standardized material culture diminished. The process was uneven, and some communities continued in smaller settlements. Proposed contributing factors include changes in river systems, shifts in monsoon patterns, ecological pressures, changing trade networks, and local social or economic transformations. No single explanation has been established as the sole cause. Evidence does not support a simple account in which one invasion alone destroyed the entire civilization.

**Significance:** Harappan urbanism demonstrates sophisticated planning, craft specialization, and interregional trade in the third millennium BCE. Its decline was a transformation into regional settlement systems, not the disappearance of all people or cultural practices.

**Related topics:** Vedic Period; prehistoric farming; ancient trade; Dholavira; Rakhigarhi; undeciphered scripts.

## 3. Vedic Period

### Overview

**Period:** Commonly divided into the Early Vedic period, roughly c. 1500–1000 BCE, and the Later Vedic period, roughly c. 1000–600 BCE. These are broad scholarly conventions, not exact boundaries.

**Location:** Early Vedic texts primarily reflect northwestern South Asia; later Vedic traditions increasingly refer to the western Ganges-Yamuna region and adjoining areas.

**Background:** The Vedic period is reconstructed mainly through texts composed and transmitted orally in Sanskrit, alongside archaeology and comparative linguistic evidence. Texts reflect particular social and ritual traditions and should not be treated as neutral descriptions of every community.

### Vedas and related texts

- **Rigveda:** A collection of hymns, among the oldest surviving Indo-Aryan texts. It invokes deities such as Indra, Agni, and Soma and reflects a world of clans, cattle wealth, ritual, and conflict.
- **Samaveda:** Organizes verses, many drawn from the Rigveda, for chanting in ritual settings.
- **Yajurveda:** Contains formulas and instructions associated with sacrificial ritual.
- **Atharvaveda:** Includes hymns, spells, healing-related material, domestic concerns, and other forms of religious expression.
- **Brahmanas:** Prose texts explaining ritual procedures and meanings.
- **Aranyakas and Upanishads:** Include reflections on ritual, knowledge, self, ultimate reality, and liberation. The early Upanishads developed within the later Vedic intellectual world.
- **Vedangas:** Auxiliary disciplines such as phonetics, grammar, meter, etymology, ritual, and astronomy/calendrical knowledge.

The Vedas were preserved through elaborate oral methods. Their transmission is historically significant, but the dates of individual compositions and layers remain debated.

### Early Vedic period

**Social and political life:** Texts describe kin-based groups and chiefs, with assemblies often called *sabha* and *samiti* appearing in the textual record. The nature and authority of these institutions are not fully clear. Social distinctions existed, but later forms of caste hierarchy should not simply be projected unchanged onto this period.

**Economy:** Cattle were an important measure of wealth. Pastoralism was prominent, alongside agriculture and exchange. Horses and chariots feature in the texts, but their presence does not mean every group lived identically.

**Religion:** Ritual offerings and hymns were directed to deities associated with natural forces, social order, and cosmic powers. Sacrifice and poetic praise were central to many elite ritual practices.

### Later Vedic period

**Political change:** Settlements and political units expanded eastward into the Ganges-Yamuna region. Larger kingdoms and more elaborate royal rituals became more prominent in texts.

**Economy:** Agriculture became increasingly important in many areas. Iron objects appear in archaeological contexts in the first millennium BCE, but the timing and social effects varied by region. Growing agrarian production supported more complex political structures.

**Society:** Textual sources show increasingly formalized social categories and ritual status distinctions. The *varna* framework appears more explicitly in later Vedic literature, but actual social life was more complex than a four-category model. Gender, labor, status, and access to ritual knowledge varied across communities and periods.

**Religion and philosophy:** Elaborate sacrifices continued, while Upanishadic texts explored questions about knowledge, the self (*atman*), ultimate reality (*brahman*), rebirth, and liberation. These ideas emerged through debate and varied schools rather than a single unified doctrine.

**Significance:** Vedic literature became foundational to several later Hindu traditions, Sanskrit learning, ritual practice, and philosophical debate. It represents one influential textual tradition among the many cultures of ancient South Asia.

**Related topics:** Mahajanapadas; Buddhism and Jainism; Sanskrit literature; early state formation.

## 4. Mahajanapadas and Religious Movements

### The sixteen Mahajanapadas

**Period:** Particularly associated with c. sixth century BCE, although the political formations developed over time.

**Location:** Mainly northern and central parts of the subcontinent.

**Background:** Urbanization, expanding agriculture, trade, coin use, and new political structures accompanied the growth of large territorial states. Buddhist and Jain texts preserve lists of sixteen *Mahajanapadas*, or “great realms.” Lists vary slightly by source.

A commonly cited list is:

1. Anga
2. Magadha
3. Kasi
4. Kosala
5. Vajji (Vrijji)
6. Malla
7. Chedi
8. Vatsa (Vamsa)
9. Kuru
10. Panchala
11. Matsya
12. Surasena
13. Assaka (Asmaka)
14. Avanti
15. Gandhara
16. Kamboja

Their borders changed over time. Some were monarchies; Vajji and certain other polities are described as *gana-sanghas*, political communities with oligarchic or assembly-based institutions. These should not automatically be equated with modern democracies.

### The rise of Magadha

**Period:** Sixth to fourth centuries BCE, culminating in the Nanda and Maurya periods.

**Location:** Southern Bihar and surrounding eastern Gangetic regions.

**Background:** Magadha benefited from fertile agricultural land, access to river routes, nearby iron resources, and the capacity to expand through war, alliances, and political consolidation.

**Key developments:** Bimbisara and Ajatashatru are associated with the expansion of Magadha in Buddhist and Jain traditions, though details and chronology vary. The kingdom absorbed or dominated neighboring territories, including Anga in traditional accounts. The Nanda dynasty preceded the Mauryas and ruled a powerful state in the fourth century BCE.

**Significance:** Magadha became the territorial and administrative base from which the Mauryan Empire emerged.

### Gautama Buddha: life and teachings

**Period:** Traditionally placed in the sixth or fifth century BCE. Exact dates remain disputed; many modern chronologies place his life in the fifth century BCE.

**Location:** Born at Lumbini, in present-day Nepal, according to Buddhist tradition; taught mainly in the Ganges plain, including places associated with Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, Rajgir, and Kushinagar.

**Background and life:** Siddhartha Gautama is remembered as a teacher who left a privileged household life in search of an end to suffering. Buddhist tradition describes his awakening at Bodh Gaya, first teaching at Sarnath, and final passing (*parinirvana*) at Kushinagar. The narrative is preserved in religious texts composed and transmitted over time; biographical details are not all independently verifiable.

**Core teachings:**
- **Four Noble Truths:** Life involves *dukkha* (unsatisfactoriness or suffering); craving and ignorance contribute to it; cessation is possible; and the Noble Eightfold Path is a way toward cessation.
- **Noble Eightfold Path:** Right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration.
- **Middle Way:** Avoidance of both extreme sensual indulgence and extreme self-mortification.
- **Impermanence (*anicca*), non-self (*anatta*), and dependent origination:** Key ideas in many Buddhist traditions, interpreted differently by later schools.
- **Ethics and monastic life:** Non-harming, compassion, disciplined conduct, meditation, and wisdom are important themes.

**Significance:** Buddhism developed into diverse traditions across Asia. Monastic institutions, pilgrimage, art, translation, and royal patronage helped its spread. See also [Mauryan Empire](#5-mauryan-empire) and [Post-Mauryan Period](#6-post-mauryan-period).

### Mahavira and Jainism

**Period:** Jain tradition places Mahavira in the sixth century BCE; scholarly chronologies vary. He is traditionally regarded as the twenty-fourth *Tirthankara* of the current Jain time cycle.

**Location:** The eastern Gangetic region, especially areas associated with present-day Bihar.

**Background and life:** Vardhamana Mahavira is remembered as a renunciant teacher who attained *kevala jnana* (omniscience in Jain belief) and taught a disciplined path of liberation. Jain traditions preserve his life story; exact dates and details are debated.

**Core teachings:** Jain traditions emphasize *ahimsa* (non-violence), truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy or sexual restraint, and non-possession. They teach that living beings possess souls (*jiva*) bound by karma and that liberation requires ethical discipline, right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct. Jain philosophies also developed ideas such as *anekantavada*, the many-sidedness of reality, and *syadvada*, qualified approaches to statements.

**Significance:** Jain communities contributed to Indian philosophy, literature, mathematics, trade, philanthropy, and temple art. Jainism developed major Digambara and Svetambara traditions, whose histories and practices differ.

### Buddhism and Jainism in context

Both traditions arose amid debates about ritual authority, renunciation, karma, rebirth, and liberation. They developed monastic and lay communities and attracted patrons from different social groups. They differ in doctrine: Buddhist traditions generally reject a permanent self, while Jainism affirms individual souls. Neither movement should be reduced to a single social or political program.

**Frequently asked questions**
- **What were the Mahajanapadas?** Sixteen major political realms described in early Buddhist and Jain sources, prominent around the sixth century BCE.
- **Who founded Buddhism?** Siddhartha Gautama, known as the Buddha, is the historical teacher associated with Buddhism.
- **Who founded Jainism?** Jain tradition identifies Mahavira as the twenty-fourth Tirthankara, not the first; Jainism traces its teachings through earlier Tirthankaras, especially Parshvanatha.
- **Why did Magadha become powerful?** Its agricultural base, river access, resources, political leadership, and military expansion supported its rise.

**Related topics:** Mauryan Empire; Ashoka; early urbanization; Buddhist councils; Jain art and literature.

## 5. Mauryan Empire

### Overview

**Period:** c. 322–185 BCE (approximate; chronologies vary slightly).

**Location:** A large empire centered on Magadha, with authority extending across much of the subcontinent at its height. The extent of direct control varied by region.

**Background:** The Mauryan Empire emerged after the Nanda dynasty and became one of the earliest large-scale imperial states in South Asian history.

### Chandragupta Maurya and the foundation of the empire

**Period:** Reign commonly dated c. 322–297 BCE.

**Background and key events:** Chandragupta Maurya founded the Mauryan dynasty after overthrowing the Nanda regime, according to later literary traditions. He expanded across northern India and confronted Seleucus I Nicator, a successor of Alexander the Great, in the late fourth century BCE. The settlement between them is commonly understood to have involved territorial arrangements and diplomatic exchange; classical sources also mention a marriage alliance, though details are debated.

**Major personalities:** Chandragupta Maurya; Seleucus I Nicator; Chanakya/Kautilya in later tradition.

**Significance:** Chandragupta established an imperial structure that his successors expanded and consolidated.

### Chanakya, Kautilya, and the Arthashastra

**Background:** Chanakya or Kautilya is traditionally identified as Chandragupta’s adviser. The *Arthashastra* is an important Sanskrit treatise on statecraft, taxation, administration, diplomacy, intelligence, and warfare.

**Historical caution:** The text survives in a form compiled and revised over time. Scholars debate its date, layers, and exact relationship to the Mauryan court. It is valuable evidence for political thought, but should not be read as a direct administrative manual proving every Mauryan practice.

### Bindusara

Bindusara succeeded Chandragupta and is usually dated to the early third century BCE. Evidence for his reign is limited compared with that for Ashoka. He appears to have maintained and possibly expanded Mauryan authority, but details are uncertain.

### Ashoka and the Kalinga War

**Period:** Ashoka’s reign is usually placed c. 268–232 BCE; dates vary. The Kalinga campaign is commonly dated to around the eighth year of his reign, often c. 261 BCE.

**Location:** Kalinga, broadly corresponding to coastal Odisha and adjoining areas.

**Key events:** Ashoka’s Major Rock Edict XIII describes the devastating human consequences of the conquest of Kalinga and expresses remorse. It connects this experience to a greater emphasis on *dhamma* and moral conquest, although the nature and timing of Ashoka’s personal transformation are interpreted differently by scholars.

**Significance:** The Kalinga War became central to Ashoka’s public presentation of kingship and his advocacy of ethical conduct.

### Ashoka’s Dhamma

Ashoka’s *dhamma* was a public ethical policy, not simply another name for Buddhist doctrine. In inscriptions, it emphasizes respect for parents and elders, humane treatment of servants, generosity, restraint, non-violence where possible, and respect among religious communities. Ashoka supported Buddhism and Buddhist institutions, but his edicts address a diverse population.

### Inscriptions and administration

Ashoka’s edicts were inscribed on rocks and pillars in Prakrit and other languages, using scripts including Brahmi and, in the northwest, Kharosthi; Greek and Aramaic versions are also known from frontier regions. The inscriptions are primary sources for royal policies and geography. James Prinsep’s decipherment of Brahmi in the nineteenth century was a major development in modern study of early Indian history.

The empire used provincial centers and officials, but the extent of uniform central control remains debated. Communication, roads, revenue, local elites, and regional variation all mattered. The empire’s exact borders and administrative divisions cannot be reconstructed in full.

### Decline and legacy

The Mauryan dynasty ended around 185 BCE, when the last ruler, Brihadratha, was killed by Pushyamitra Shunga according to later accounts. The reasons for the empire’s fragmentation likely included succession issues, regional political dynamics, and the difficulty of maintaining a large state; simple explanations such as Ashoka’s non-violence alone are inadequate.

**Related topics:** Kalinga; Buddhism; inscriptions; Shunga dynasty; ancient state formation.

**Frequently asked questions**
- **Who founded the Mauryan Empire?** Chandragupta Maurya, around 322 BCE.
- **Who was Chanakya?** A figure in tradition associated with Chandragupta’s rise and with political wisdom; the *Arthashastra* is attributed to Kautilya, though its textual history is complex.
- **Why is Ashoka important?** His inscriptions provide unusually direct evidence of an ancient ruler’s public policies, and his support for Buddhist institutions and ethical governance had a lasting legacy.

## 6. Post-Mauryan Period

### Overview

**Period:** Broadly c. 200 BCE–300 CE, with regional timelines extending beyond these dates.

**Location:** Northern India, the northwest, the Deccan, and southern India.

**Background:** After Mauryan imperial authority weakened, multiple dynasties and regional states developed. Trade across the Indian Ocean and overland routes linked South Asia to Central Asia, the Mediterranean world, and Southeast Asia.

### Shungas and Kanvas

The Shunga dynasty emerged in Magadha around 185 BCE, traditionally associated with Pushyamitra Shunga. Its political reach and policies are debated, and the evidence does not support simplistic claims that the dynasty uniformly ended Buddhist patronage. Buddhist monuments such as the Great Stupa at Sanchi underwent later enlargement and embellishment across different periods. The Kanvas succeeded the Shungas in parts of the Gangetic region before the rise of other powers.

### Satavahanas

**Period:** Dates are debated; the dynasty is generally placed between the late first millennium BCE and the third century CE.

**Location:** The Deccan, with changing centers and influence across parts of present-day Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and adjoining regions.

**Background and significance:** The Satavahanas supported trade, issued coins, and interacted with merchants, religious institutions, and neighboring powers. Inscriptions and donations show patronage of Buddhist caves and monasteries, while Brahmanical practices also received support. Gautamiputra Satakarni is a prominent ruler known from inscriptions and coins. The dynasty’s chronology and territorial extent remain contested.

### Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians

- **Indo-Greeks:** Greek-speaking rulers established kingdoms in parts of the northwest after the breakup of Hellenistic power in Central Asia. Menander (Milinda) is known in Buddhist literature as a king who debated the monk Nagasena, though the literary account is not a straightforward contemporary transcript.
- **Shakas (Indo-Scythians):** Groups of Central Asian origin ruled parts of western and northern India. The Western Kshatrapas, including Rudradaman I, are known for inscriptions and regional political power.
- **Indo-Parthians:** Rulers such as Gondophares held territory in the northwest. Their chronology and connections with neighboring states are reconstructed from coins and inscriptions.

These states show cultural interaction and political mobility across Central Asia and South Asia. “Foreign” and “Indian” identities were not fixed categories; rulers adapted to local institutions, languages, and religious settings.

### Kushana Empire and Kanishka

**Period:** Kushana power flourished mainly in the first to third centuries CE. The date of Kanishka’s accession is disputed; proposed dates have ranged, and no single chronology should be treated as certain.

**Location:** Central Asia, Afghanistan, the northwest, and parts of northern India, with boundaries changing over time.

**Background and developments:** The Kushanas connected Central Asian and South Asian networks. Kanishka is associated with patronage of Buddhism, but the empire was religiously diverse. Coins depict a range of deities, reflecting plural religious traditions. Gandharan art combined South Asian and Hellenistic artistic elements, while Mathura developed its own important sculptural style.

**Buddhism:** Kanishka is traditionally linked to a Buddhist council, often associated with Kashmir, but accounts differ and the historicity and details are debated. Kushana-era trade and patronage helped Buddhist institutions and art flourish along routes connecting South Asia with Central Asia and China.

### Trade routes and cultural exchanges

The subcontinent was connected through overland networks across the northwest and Central Asia and maritime routes across the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Roman-era trade involved commodities such as spices, textiles, beads, gems, and other goods; imports included coins, glassware, and wine in some contexts. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a Greek-language work from the first century CE, describes ports and trade in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean world. Trade was not only commercial: it also moved religious ideas, artistic forms, technologies, and languages.

**Related topics:** Gupta Empire; Gandhara; Mathura; early Tamil literature; Indian Ocean trade.

## 7. Gupta Empire

### Overview

**Period:** The Gupta dynasty rose in the late third or early fourth century CE and was prominent in northern India through the fifth century; its later authority declined in the sixth century. Exact boundaries and dates vary.

**Location:** The Gangetic plain and parts of central and northern India, with changing degrees of influence.

**Background:** Gupta rulers created a powerful dynasty after earlier regional states. Inscriptions, coins, literary works, and archaeology are key sources, though each has limitations.

### Major rulers

- **Chandragupta I:** Usually dated to the early fourth century CE. His marriage alliance with a Licchavi princess is highlighted in Gupta inscriptions and coinage; the precise political implications are debated.
- **Samudragupta:** Known especially from the Allahabad Pillar inscription (also called the Prayaga Prashasti), composed by his court poet Harisena. It records campaigns and relationships with different rulers. The inscription is a royal eulogy and must be read critically rather than as a neutral military report.
- **Chandragupta II:** Associated with the expansion of Gupta influence and a period of artistic and commercial activity. His reign is linked to the defeat or subordination of Western Kshatrapa power in western India, though details of the campaigns are reconstructed from multiple sources.

### Administration and economy

Gupta administration combined royal authority with provincial, district, and local officials. Land grants to religious institutions and individuals became increasingly important in some regions. Such grants could transfer revenue rights and influence local power structures. This development varied regionally and is part of wider debates about early medieval political economy.

Gold coins are a notable feature of Gupta-era material culture, but coin use and monetization varied across regions and periods. Agriculture, crafts, local markets, and long-distance trade all contributed to the economy.

### Literature, mathematics, astronomy, and art

- **Kalidasa:** A major Sanskrit poet and dramatist traditionally associated with the Gupta age, although his exact dates and court connections are uncertain. Works attributed to him include *Abhijnanashakuntalam* and *Meghaduta*.
- **Aryabhata:** Born in 476 CE, he composed the *Aryabhatiya* in 499 CE. His work includes mathematical methods and astronomical calculations. He explained the apparent daily rotation of the heavens through Earth’s rotation and made important contributions to place-value calculation and trigonometry, though historical terminology differs from modern usage.
- **Varahamihira:** A sixth-century astronomer and scholar associated with Ujjain; his works synthesize astronomical and other learned traditions.
- **Sanskrit learning:** Court poetry, drama, religious literature, and technical scholarship flourished, but regional languages and oral traditions also continued.
- **Art and architecture:** Gupta and related-period art includes refined sculpture, temple building, and developments in Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain imagery. The surviving evidence is regionally varied.

### Was the Gupta period a single “Golden Age”?

The term “Golden Age” is used because of achievements in Sanskrit literature, mathematics, astronomy, sculpture, and political power. However, it can obscure uneven development. The surviving record disproportionately reflects courts, elite texts, and religious institutions. Social hierarchy, gender inequalities, labor conditions, regional variation, and the experiences of non-elite communities complicate a uniformly celebratory account. Scientific and artistic advances also built on earlier knowledge and continued beyond Gupta rule.

**Decline:** Gupta authority weakened through succession struggles, regional assertions of power, and incursions by groups described in sources as Hunas. The dynasty did not vanish everywhere at once; regional rulers continued to claim Gupta connections or traditions.

**Related topics:** Aryabhata; Kalidasa; Ajanta; early medieval regional states; land grants.

**Frequently asked question:** **Who was Samudragupta?** A major Gupta ruler remembered for military campaigns and political alliances recorded in the Allahabad Pillar inscription.

## 8. Early Medieval India

### Overview

**Period:** Roughly sixth to thirteenth centuries CE; boundaries differ by region and topic.

**Location:** The subcontinent, with powerful regional courts in the north, Deccan, and south.

**Background:** This period saw the rise and competition of regional dynasties, changing agrarian systems, temple-centered institutions, regional literary cultures, and expanding maritime networks. “Early medieval” is a useful period label but does not imply that all regions followed one path.

### Harsha and northern India

**Period:** Harsha ruled c. 606–647 CE.

**Location:** His power centered on north India, especially areas around Thanesar and Kannauj, with influence varying by region.

**Background and events:** Harsha consolidated power after a period of political conflict and became a major ruler in the northern plains. Xuanzang, a Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who traveled in India in the seventh century, described aspects of his reign and religious institutions. Harsha’s authority did not extend uniformly over all of India. His southern campaign was checked by the Chalukya ruler Pulakeshin II, according to inscriptional evidence.

**Significance:** Harsha patronized religious and literary activity and is associated with Sanskrit works such as *Nagananda*, *Ratnavali*, and *Priyadarsika*, though authorship traditions and historical details require care.

### Chalukyas

**Period:** The Badami Chalukyas were prominent from the sixth to eighth centuries; later Chalukya lines ruled in other periods.

**Location:** The Deccan, especially Karnataka and adjoining regions.

**Major rulers:** Pulakeshin II, known for his conflict with Harsha and campaigns across the Deccan; Vikramaditya I and successors.

**Architecture and significance:** Badami, Aihole, and Pattadakal preserve important examples of rock-cut and structural temple architecture. Chalukya-era builders experimented with forms that shaped later Deccan temple traditions.

### Pallavas

**Period:** Particularly prominent from the fourth to ninth centuries CE.

**Location:** Northern Tamil Nadu and adjoining parts of southeastern India, with Kanchipuram as an important center.

**Major rulers:** Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I are prominent in inscriptions and architectural history.

**Architecture:** Pallava sites at Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram) include rock-cut caves, monolithic rathas, relief sculpture, and structural temples. Pallava artistic forms influenced later southern Indian temple architecture and regional connections across the Bay of Bengal.

### Rashtrakutas

**Period:** c. eighth to tenth centuries CE.

**Location:** The Deccan, with power extending into parts of northern and southern India at different times.

**Major rulers:** Dantidurga, Krishna I, and Amoghavarsha I.

**Significance:** Rashtrakutas competed with the Pratiharas and Palas in the struggle for Kannauj, a political center in north India. Their rule supported literature in Sanskrit and Kannada. The Kailasa temple at Ellora, usually associated with Krishna I, is a major rock-cut monument, though the complex developed over time.

### Cholas: Rajaraja I and Rajendra I

**Period:** The imperial Cholas were especially powerful from the ninth to thirteenth centuries CE.

**Location:** The Kaveri delta and Tamil region, with influence at various times across Sri Lanka and maritime Southeast Asia.

**Major rulers:**
- **Rajaraja I (r. c. 985–1014):** Expanded Chola power and commissioned the Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur, completed around 1010.
- **Rajendra I (r. c. 1014–1044):** Extended campaigns into parts of eastern India and led a naval expedition against Srivijaya-linked centers in Southeast Asia, demonstrating the reach of Chola maritime power. The precise aims and consequences of the expedition are debated.

**Administration:** Chola inscriptions document royal officials, local assemblies, temple endowments, irrigation, land revenue, and village institutions. Local self-government was not universal or equal; participation and power were shaped by property, caste, and local status.

**Economy and culture:** Irrigation and agrarian production supported temples, towns, crafts, and maritime exchange. Tamil literature, bronze sculpture, devotional traditions, and monumental architecture flourished.

### Other important regional powers

- **Palas (eighth–twelfth centuries):** Ruled parts of Bengal and Bihar and supported Buddhist institutions, including Nalanda and Vikramashila in different periods. Pala sculpture and manuscript traditions influenced Buddhist art beyond India.
- **Gurjara-Pratiharas (roughly eighth–eleventh centuries):** A major north Indian power and one participant in the tripartite struggle over Kannauj.
- **Eastern Gangas:** Ruled parts of Odisha and are associated with the Jagannath tradition and the Konark Sun Temple, built later under Narasimhadeva I in the thirteenth century.
- **Paramaras, Chandelas, and other central Indian dynasties:** Supported regional courts, temples, and literary traditions. Khajuraho’s temples were built under Chandela patronage, mainly between the tenth and eleventh centuries.
- **Kashmir and Himalayan polities:** Developed distinct political and artistic traditions connected to broader South and Central Asian networks.

**Significance:** Early medieval India was characterized by strong regional cultures, temple economies, literary production, and interconnected trade. Political power was fragmented in many periods, but fragmentation did not mean cultural isolation.

**Related topics:** Bhakti; regional languages; temple architecture; Indian Ocean trade; Delhi Sultanate.

## 9. Delhi Sultanate

### Overview

**Period:** 1206–1526 CE.

**Location:** Centered on Delhi, with territories that expanded and contracted across northern India and, under some rulers, into the Deccan.

**Background:** The Delhi Sultanate comprised five major ruling dynasties conventionally identified as the Mamluk (often called Slave), Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, and Lodi dynasties. Political authority varied across time and territory; local rulers, zamindars, and regional powers remained important.

### Mamluk dynasty (1206–1290)

- **Qutb al-Din Aibak:** Established independent rule in north India after the Ghurid empire’s fragmentation. He is associated with early construction at the Qutb complex.
- **Iltutmish:** Consolidated the sultanate, strengthened its institutions, and is associated with the development of the Delhi-centered state and the use of the silver tanka.
- **Razia Sultan:** Ruled in 1236–1240 and was one of the most prominent women to rule the Delhi Sultanate. Her authority faced opposition from powerful elites.
- **Balban:** Strengthened royal authority and court discipline.

### Khalji dynasty (1290–1320)

**Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296–1316):** Expanded the sultanate and resisted Mongol incursions. His campaigns into the Deccan and western India extended political influence, though local control varied. He introduced market regulations and revenue measures intended partly to support the army and state. Their implementation and impact differed across regions, and sources often reflect court perspectives.

### Tughlaq dynasty (1320–1414)

- **Ghiyas al-Din Tughlaq:** Founded the dynasty.
- **Muhammad bin Tughlaq:** Known for ambitious policies, including the attempted transfer of the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad and experiments with token currency. The outcomes were mixed; later accounts often portray these policies as failures, but the administrative context and details require critical reading.
- **Firuz Shah Tughlaq:** Built canals, towns, and public works and issued administrative measures. His rule also involved forms of religious policy and social hierarchy that need to be understood in their historical context.
- **Timur’s invasion (1398):** Timur’s forces sacked Delhi, severely damaging the city and weakening the sultanate’s authority.

### Sayyid dynasty (1414–1451) and Lodi dynasty (1451–1526)

The Sayyids ruled a comparatively restricted territory after the Tughlaq period. The Lodi dynasty, of Afghan origin, attempted to strengthen the state. Ibrahim Lodi was defeated by Babur at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526, conventionally marking the end of the Delhi Sultanate and the beginning of Mughal rule.

### Administration, economy, and culture

The sultanate used revenue collection, military service, court offices, and assignments of revenue or land rights, with systems changing over time. Persian was an important language of administration and high culture, while Arabic, Sanskrit, and many regional languages continued in different settings. Indo-Islamic architecture combined and transformed local and transregional building practices. Examples include the Qutb Minar complex, Alai Darwaza, Tughlaqabad, and later Lodi-era tombs.

Urban centers, crafts, agriculture, and trade supported the state. The period included both conflict and cooperation among rulers, local elites, religious communities, and merchants. It cannot be accurately summarized as either uniform tolerance or uniform persecution.

**Significance:** The Delhi Sultanate reshaped political institutions, military organization, urban life, architecture, and Persianate culture in northern India, while regional powers remained influential.

**Related topics:** Bhakti and Sufi movements; Vijayanagara and Bahmani kingdoms; Mughal Empire; Indo-Islamic architecture.

## 10. Mughal Empire

### Overview

**Period:** Mughal rule began in 1526. Imperial authority was strongest from the sixteenth through much of the seventeenth century, then weakened unevenly during the eighteenth century. The last Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah II (Bahadur Shah Zafar), was deposed by the British after the Revolt of 1857.

**Location:** Based in northern India, with the empire at different times covering much of the subcontinent. Regional control varied substantially.

### Babur (r. 1526–1530)

Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur, a Central Asian ruler descended from Timur and related through lineage to the Chinggisid world, defeated Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526. He also defeated a Rajput confederacy associated with Rana Sanga at Khanwa in 1527. Babur’s memoir, the *Baburnama*, is a valuable source for his life, campaigns, and observations, though it reflects his perspective.

### Humayun and Sher Shah Suri

Humayun (r. 1530–1540 and 1555–1556) lost power to Sher Shah Suri, an Afghan ruler, and later recovered Delhi with Persian Safavid support. Sher Shah ruled from 1540 to 1545 and is associated with administrative and revenue reforms, improvements to roads and communications, and the issue of a silver coin commonly called the *rupiya*. Later Mughal institutions drew on earlier practices as well as their own innovations; claims that Sher Shah alone invented every later system are too simple.

### Akbar (r. 1556–1605)

**Background:** Akbar consolidated and expanded the empire after ascending the throne as a teenager.

**Policies and developments:** His administration used the *mansabdari* system, ranking officials and military obligations. Revenue administration developed through the work of officials such as Raja Todar Mal, with measurement and assessment practices that varied regionally. Akbar pursued alliances with several Rajput houses while also fighting other regional powers. His court sponsored translation, debate, painting, architecture, and historical writing.

Akbar’s *sulh-i kul* (“universal peace”) is often used to describe an ideal of broad political accommodation. His religious policies changed over time and should be understood through specific measures, court debates, and political circumstances rather than a single label.

### Jahangir (r. 1605–1627)

Jahangir continued Mughal administration and patronage of painting. His memoir, the *Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri*, is an important source. Nur Jahan, his wife, exercised significant political influence and participated in court politics, demonstrating the role some royal women played in governance.

### Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658)

Shah Jahan’s reign is associated with monumental architecture, including the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort in Delhi, and the Jama Masjid in Delhi. The Taj Mahal was built at Agra as the mausoleum of Mumtaz Mahal. The period also saw courtly splendor and military campaigns, alongside heavy demands on revenue and labor.

### Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707)

Aurangzeb expanded Mughal territory, especially through prolonged campaigns in the Deccan, but these wars placed severe strain on the imperial treasury and military system. His reign included the reimposition of the *jizya* tax on non-Muslims in 1679, as well as policies and conflicts that varied by region. His relations with Rajput houses, Marathas, Sikhs, Deccan sultanates, and other groups were complex and changed over time. Explanations of Mughal decline should not be reduced to his religious policy alone.

### Mansabdari and revenue systems

- **Mansab:** A rank in Mughal service that indicated an official’s status and, in combination with related obligations, military responsibilities.
- **Jagir:** A revenue assignment given to an official in lieu of direct cash payment. A jagir was generally not the same as private ownership of the land.
- **Zabt and related revenue practices:** Systems for assessing and collecting agrarian revenue, associated especially with Akbar’s period and the work of Todar Mal. Their reach and operation varied regionally.
- **Zamindars:** Local intermediaries or landed authorities whose roles differed across regions and periods; they could collect revenue, hold local power, and sometimes resist imperial demands.

### Art, literature, and architecture

Mughal painting developed through the interaction of Persianate and South Asian traditions, with distinctive styles under Akbar, Jahangir, and Shah Jahan. Court histories such as the *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari* are important sources, although they are shaped by imperial perspectives. Architecture includes forts, gardens, mosques, tombs, and palaces. Persian was a major court language, while Hindi, Braj, Urdu/Hindustani, Bengali, Punjabi, and other regional literary traditions developed in varied contexts.

### Relations with regional powers and decline

The Mughal Empire negotiated, fought, and formed alliances with Rajputs, Marathas, Sikhs, Deccan states, and other regional forces. After Aurangzeb’s death in 1707, succession struggles, regional autonomy, fiscal and military pressures, and external invasions weakened central authority. Nadir Shah’s invasion and sack of Delhi in 1739 was a major shock. The Marathas, Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad, Mysore, and other states became influential. The East India Company gradually replaced Mughal authority in many areas. In 1857, rebels invoked Bahadur Shah Zafar as a symbolic sovereign; the British deposed him after suppressing the uprising.

**Frequently asked questions**
- **Who founded the Mughal Empire?** Babur, after his victory at Panipat in 1526.
- **Who built the Taj Mahal?** It was commissioned by Shah Jahan in memory of Mumtaz Mahal.
- **What was the mansabdari system?** A Mughal system of ranking officials and linking rank to service and military obligations.
- **Why did Mughal authority decline?** A combination of succession disputes, fiscal and military strains, regional state-building, invasions, and changing political-economic conditions.

**Related topics:** Sher Shah Suri; Maratha power; Sikh history; European colonial expansion.

## 11. Bhakti and Sufi Movements

### Overview

**Period:** Bhakti traditions developed over many centuries, with major regional movements from the early medieval period onward. Sufi orders became influential in South Asia especially from the medieval period.

**Location:** Across the subcontinent, with distinct Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Bengali, Hindi, Punjabi, Kashmiri, and other regional traditions.

**Background:** “Bhakti” broadly refers to devotional approaches to a deity. “Sufism” refers to diverse Islamic spiritual traditions emphasizing inward discipline, remembrance of God, ethical conduct, and teacher-disciple relationships. Neither was a single centralized movement.

### Bhakti traditions and important figures

- **Alvars:** Tamil Vaishnava poet-saints, active across the early medieval period, whose hymns praised Vishnu and became central to Tamil devotional literature.
- **Nayanars:** Tamil Shaiva poet-saints who composed hymns in praise of Shiva.
- **Basavanna (Basava):** A twelfth-century reformer associated with the Virashaiva/Lingayat tradition in Karnataka. The movement emphasized devotion to Shiva and challenged some established social and ritual hierarchies, though its later history is diverse.
- **Akka Mahadevi:** A Kannada-language poet and mystic associated with the Virashaiva tradition.
- **Ramanuja:** An influential theologian associated with Vishishtadvaita Vedanta and Sri Vaishnava traditions.
- **Madhva:** Associated with Dvaita Vedanta.
- **Namdev and Tukaram:** Marathi devotional poets associated with Varkari traditions and devotion to Vithoba at Pandharpur.
- **Kabir:** A poet whose verses challenge religious pretension and emphasize devotion to a formless divine. His life dates and the history of individual verses are not always certain.
- **Ravidas (Raidas):** A devotional poet remembered in traditions that emphasize spiritual equality and criticize caste-based exclusion.
- **Mirabai:** A Rajput woman and poet associated with devotion to Krishna; biographical traditions vary.
- **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu:** A fifteenth–sixteenth-century Bengali Vaishnava leader associated with ecstatic devotion to Krishna.
- **Guru Nanak:** Founder of the Sikh tradition, whose teachings emphasized devotion to one God, ethical living, and social equality.

### Sufi traditions

Sufi communities in South Asia included several *silsilas* (spiritual lineages or orders). The Chishti order became influential in north India; prominent figures include Muin al-Din Chishti of Ajmer, Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, Baba Farid, Nizamuddin Auliya, and Amir Khusrau, a poet and musician associated with the Delhi court and Sufi circles. The Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi, and other orders also developed in different periods and regions.

Sufi shrines and hospices could serve as places of teaching, hospitality, devotion, and social exchange. Practices varied, and Sufi traditions sometimes debated music, ritual, authority, and relations with rulers.

### Ideas, literature, and social influence

Bhakti and Sufi traditions often used vernacular languages and poetry, making religious ideas accessible beyond elite scholarly settings. Some figures criticized caste hierarchy, ritual formalism, or religious exclusivity, but the movements did not uniformly abolish social inequalities. Their teachings were preserved by communities that sometimes interpreted the same figure differently.

**Significance:** These traditions shaped music, poetry, pilgrimage, religious practice, regional languages, and intercommunity exchange. They also reveal the diversity within Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and other South Asian religious histories.

**Related topics:** Sikh history; regional literature; medieval urban life; temple and shrine cultures.

## 12. Rise of Regional Powers

### Vijayanagara Empire

**Period:** c. 1336–1646, though the empire’s power and successor states changed over time.

**Location:** Southern India, with its capital at Vijayanagara near present-day Hampi in Karnataka.

**Background and rulers:** The empire is traditionally associated with Harihara I and Bukka Raya I of the Sangama dynasty. Later dynasties included the Saluvas, Tuluvas, and Aravidus. Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) was one of its most prominent rulers, associated with military campaigns, courtly literature, temple patronage, and irrigation works.

**Economy and culture:** Vijayanagara linked agriculture, markets, crafts, and long-distance trade. Its capital included monumental temples, bazaars, palaces, and water systems. The empire interacted with Portuguese traders and rival Deccan sultanates.

**Battle of Talikota (1565):** A coalition of Deccan sultanates defeated Vijayanagara forces. The capital was devastated, but the empire did not instantly disappear; successor rulers continued elsewhere.

### Bahmani kingdom and Deccan sultanates

**Period:** The Bahmani Sultanate was founded in 1347 and fragmented into successor states during the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries.

**Location:** The Deccan plateau.

**Successor states:** Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, Golconda, Bidar, and Berar are commonly grouped as the Deccan sultanates. They competed and formed shifting alliances with one another, Vijayanagara, and northern powers. They supported Persianate and Deccani literary cultures, architecture, painting, and music. Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur and the Qutb Shahi monuments at Hyderabad are examples of later Deccan architectural traditions.

### Maratha power and Shivaji

**Period:** Shivaji lived c. 1630–1680; the Maratha polity expanded in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

**Location:** Western India, especially the Sahyadri region and the Deccan, later extending across large parts of the subcontinent.

**Background and events:** Shivaji built a regional state through fort networks, military organization, diplomacy, and campaigns against the Deccan sultanates and Mughal forces. He was crowned at Raigad in 1674. His administration included the *Ashta Pradhan* council in traditional accounts, though its actual role and evolution require careful historical analysis. Maratha forces used mobile warfare and drew revenue through systems including *chauth* and *sardeshmukhi* in territories where they could enforce them.

**After Shivaji:** The Peshwas became increasingly influential. Maratha confederate houses included the Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads, and Bhonsles. The Third Battle of Panipat in 1761 against Ahmad Shah Durrani’s forces was a major defeat, but Maratha power recovered and remained central to eighteenth-century politics before defeat by the British in the Anglo-Maratha Wars.

### Sikh history and the Sikh Empire

**Period:** Sikh tradition began with Guru Nanak (1469–1539) and developed through ten human Gurus, followed by the Guru Granth Sahib as the eternal Guru in Sikh belief. The Sikh Empire under Maharaja Ranjit Singh flourished from 1799 to 1849.

**Location:** Punjab and adjoining northwestern regions.

**Key developments:** Guru Nanak emphasized devotion to one God, truthful living, service, and equality. Later Gurus developed Sikh institutions and community identity. Guru Arjan compiled an early form of the Adi Granth and was executed in 1606 under Mughal authority. Guru Hargobind developed a tradition of temporal and spiritual leadership; Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed in 1675. Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699 and declared the Guru Granth Sahib the continuing Guru.

The eighteenth century saw Sikh misls (confederacies) and conflict with Mughal and Afghan powers. Ranjit Singh unified much of Punjab and built a state that included Sikh, Muslim, Hindu, and European officials and soldiers. After his death in 1839, internal struggles weakened the state. The Anglo-Sikh Wars led to British annexation of Punjab in 1849.

### Mysore, Rajputs, and other regional states

- **Mysore:** The Wodeyar dynasty ruled from the region of Mysore. Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan expanded the state and fought the British East India Company in the Anglo-Mysore Wars. Tipu Sultan died at Seringapatam in 1799 during the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War.
- **Rajput states:** Rajput dynasties ruled areas of Rajasthan and adjoining regions across many centuries. Their relationships with the Delhi Sultanate, Mughals, Marathas, and British varied from warfare to alliances and service.
- **Awadh, Bengal, and Hyderabad:** These states gained substantial autonomy as Mughal authority weakened. Bengal was taken over by the East India Company after mid-eighteenth-century conflicts; Hyderabad remained a princely state under British paramountcy until 1948.
- **Travancore and other southern states:** Regional rulers supported administration, trade, temple institutions, and distinctive cultural traditions, while negotiating European power.

**Significance:** Regional states were not merely transitional actors between empires. They shaped language, institutions, military practices, architecture, and trade, and were central to the political history of the eighteenth century.

## 13. European Arrival and Colonial Expansion

### European trading powers

**Period:** Portuguese arrival by sea in 1498; European commercial and political competition intensified during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries.

**Background:** Indian Ocean commerce already connected South Asia to East Africa, Arabia, the Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia, and China. Europeans entered established networks rather than discovering an empty or isolated trading world.

- **Portuguese:** Vasco da Gama reached Calicut (Kozhikode) in 1498. The Portuguese established fortified trading posts and naval influence in parts of the western coast and Indian Ocean, including Goa, captured in 1510.
- **Dutch:** The Dutch East India Company (VOC), founded in 1602, traded in textiles, spices, and other goods. Its influence in India was concentrated in selected ports and commercial networks.
- **English:** The English East India Company was chartered in 1600. It developed factories and settlements, including Surat, Madras (Chennai), Bombay (Mumbai), and Calcutta (Kolkata).
- **French:** The French East India Company was founded in 1664. French settlements included Pondicherry (Puducherry), Chandernagore, and other posts. French and British companies competed in the Carnatic and elsewhere.

### From commerce to territorial power

European companies depended on local rulers, merchants, bankers, soldiers, and political alliances. Company rule did not arise automatically from trade; it grew through military victories, revenue rights, diplomacy, and exploitation of political rivalries.

### Battle of Plassey (1757)

**Location:** Bengal, near Palashi.

**Background and events:** East India Company forces led by Robert Clive defeated the army of Nawab Siraj ud-Daulah in a battle shaped by defections and elite political negotiations, including the role of Mir Jafar. Plassey strengthened Company influence in Bengal but was not, by itself, the completion of British conquest of India.

**Significance:** The Company gained decisive political leverage and access to Bengal’s wealth, helping finance further expansion.

### Battle of Buxar (1764)

**Location:** Present-day Bihar.

**Background and events:** Company forces defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, the Nawab of Awadh Shuja-ud-Daula, and Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. The Treaty of Allahabad in 1765 granted the Company the *diwani*, or revenue-collection rights, for Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa (the historical term used in the treaty context).

**Significance:** Buxar and the 1765 revenue settlement institutionalized Company control over major revenues and strengthened its position as a territorial power.

### Major colonial policies and institutions

- **Permanent Settlement (1793):** Introduced under Governor-General Lord Cornwallis in Bengal and certain adjoining areas. It fixed the state revenue demand on zamindars, with important consequences for landholding and peasant relations.
- **Ryotwari systems:** Associated with officials such as Thomas Munro and used in parts of southern and western India. Revenue was assessed with cultivators, though practice varied.
- **Mahalwari system:** Used in parts of northern India, with revenue assessment linked to village estates or *mahals*.
- **Subsidiary Alliance:** Associated with Lord Wellesley, it required allied rulers to accept British troops and constraints on external relations.
- **Doctrine of Lapse:** Associated with Lord Dalhousie; the Company annexed certain princely states when rulers died without a recognized natural male heir, rejecting some adoptions for succession purposes. It was one among several annexation policies.
- **Education and administration:** English-language education, universities founded in 1857, codified law, railways, telegraph, and postal networks changed society and governance. These developments served colonial priorities but were also used by Indians for education, mobility, political organization, and communication.
- **Economic effects:** Colonial rule redirected revenue and trade, altered land relations, promoted export-oriented production in some sectors, and contributed to the decline or restructuring of some crafts. The scale and causes of deindustrialization, famine, and poverty are debated in scholarship, but colonial extraction and policy failures were important factors in many regions.
- **Famine and public health:** Major famines occurred under colonial rule, including the Bengal famine of 1770 and the Great Famine of 1876–1878. Causes involved drought and ecological stress as well as policy, market structures, and relief failures; explanations should be specific to each famine.

### Resistance to colonial expansion

Resistance took many forms: local rebellions, peasant protests, tribal uprisings, princely resistance, religious reform, and organized nationalism. Examples include the Sannyasi-Fakir resistance, the Paika rebellion of 1817 (whose scope and interpretation are debated), the Santhal rebellion of 1855–1856, the Indigo revolt of 1859–1860, and numerous regional movements. Not all resistance had the same goals or a single national program.

**Related topics:** Revolt of 1857; Indian National Congress; economic nationalism; princely states.

## 14. Indian Revolt of 1857

### Overview

**Period:** The uprising began in May 1857 and was suppressed unevenly through 1858, with resistance continuing in some places afterward.

**Location:** Major centers included Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Gwalior, Bareilly, and parts of central and northern India. Participation varied widely by region.

### Causes

- **Military grievances:** Sepoys in the Company’s Bengal Army faced pay, service, promotion, and cultural grievances. The new Enfield rifle cartridge became a trigger because rumors held that cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to many Hindu and Muslim soldiers. The cartridge issue was a catalyst within a broader crisis.
- **Political annexation:** The annexation of Awadh in 1856 and other policies alienated rulers, court elites, landholders, and soldiers.
- **Economic pressures:** Revenue demands, changes in landholding, and disruptions to livelihoods contributed to discontent in some areas.
- **Religious and cultural fears:** Some Indians feared interference with established practices and social institutions. These concerns varied across communities and cannot be reduced to a single motive.

### Major events and leaders

- **10 May 1857, Meerut:** Sepoys rebelled and moved toward Delhi.
- **Delhi:** Rebels proclaimed the elderly Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as a symbolic leader.
- **Kanpur:** Nana Sahib became a leading figure in the uprising.
- **Lucknow and Awadh:** Begum Hazrat Mahal and other leaders resisted British forces.
- **Jhansi and central India:** Rani Lakshmibai became a prominent military leader; Tantia Tope (Tatya Tope) continued resistance in central India.
- **Bihar:** Kunwar Singh, a landholder from Jagdishpur, led resistance.
- **Other areas:** Bakht Khan played a military role in Delhi; participation included soldiers, peasants, artisans, local rulers, and others, but many regions remained outside the uprising or supported the British.

### Why did the revolt fail?

The uprising lacked unified command, consistent aims, and coordinated resources across regions. Rebel forces faced logistical and military disadvantages, while many princely states, communities, and soldiers did not join or actively supported the British. British forces used reinforcements and superior coordination in key campaigns. These factors varied by location.

### Consequences

- The East India Company’s rule ended under the Government of India Act 1858, and authority transferred to the British Crown.
- The governor-general also became the viceroy in the new imperial framework.
- The British reorganized the army and adjusted recruitment, seeking to reduce the risk of coordinated rebellion.
- The Crown’s proclamation promised non-interference in religious matters and respect for princely treaties, though colonial control continued.
- The Mughal dynasty ended; Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to Rangoon (Yangon), where he died in 1862.

### Historical interpretations

The uprising has been described as a sepoy mutiny, a popular rebellion, a regional anti-colonial revolt, and—especially in nationalist interpretation—the First War of Indian Independence. No single label captures every participant’s motives or every region’s experience. The term “Revolt of 1857” is a useful broad designation.

**Related topics:** Company rule; colonial army; princely states; Indian nationalism.

**Frequently asked question:** **What were the causes of the Revolt of 1857?** Military grievances and the cartridge controversy acted as triggers amid political annexation, economic disruption, and cultural-religious anxieties.

## 15. Indian National Movement

### Overview

**Period:** Organized nationalism grew in the late nineteenth century and developed into a mass anti-colonial movement in the twentieth century, culminating in independence in 1947.

**Location:** Across British India and princely states, with different regional organizations and political aims.

**Background:** Nationalism emerged from multiple sources: colonial economic and political structures, modern education and print culture, social reform, regional associations, labor and peasant struggles, religious and cultural movements, and global ideas about rights and self-government.

### Formation of the Indian National Congress

The Indian National Congress (INC) was founded in Bombay in December 1885. A. O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, helped organize the first meeting, whose president was Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee. Early Congress leaders sought greater Indian participation in government, civil rights, and administrative reform. Its social base was initially limited and disproportionately educated and urban, but the organization later became a major platform for mass nationalism.

### Moderates and Extremists

- **Moderates:** Leaders such as Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, and Pherozeshah Mehta used petitions, public debate, and constitutional methods. Naoroji’s “drain of wealth” critique argued that colonial economic arrangements transferred wealth from India to Britain.
- **Extremists or assertive nationalists:** Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, and Lala Lajpat Rai emphasized swadeshi, boycott, and more forceful mass political action. The terms reflect political divisions of the period and should not be treated as fixed identities across every issue.

### Swadeshi Movement (1905 onward)

The 1905 partition of Bengal by the British government triggered protests, boycott of foreign goods, promotion of indigenous industry, national education initiatives, and public mobilization. The movement involved students, women, urban groups, and regional organizations, though participation and political positions varied. The partition was annulled in 1911, while Bengal’s administrative boundaries were reorganized.

### Home Rule Movement (1916–1918)

Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant led Home Rule leagues seeking self-government within the British Empire. The movement built political networks and helped revive nationalist activity during the First World War. Its momentum was overtaken by wider mass politics after 1919.

### Revolutionary movements

Revolutionary nationalists used secret societies, political assassinations, armed actions, and attempts to obtain weapons. Figures and organizations included Khudiram Bose, Prafulla Chaki, the Ghadar movement, Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA), Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, and others. Their motives and methods differed. Bhagat Singh and colleagues also used trials and writings to publicize anti-colonial and socialist ideas. Revolutionary activity was one strand of a much wider movement.

### Gandhi and mass politics

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in 1915. He developed *satyagraha*, a method of political action emphasizing truth, non-violent resistance, and willingness to accept suffering. Gandhi’s movements depended on local leaders and participants; they were not solely the work of one person.

#### Champaran Satyagraha (1917)

**Location:** Champaran, Bihar. Gandhi supported peasants compelled under indigo cultivation arrangements and helped secure an inquiry and reforms. The campaign became an important early example of his methods in India.

#### Kheda Satyagraha (1918)

**Location:** Kheda district, Gujarat. Peasants sought revenue relief during crop failure and hardship. Gandhi, Vallabhbhai Patel, and local organizers supported the campaign; outcomes were partial and shaped by negotiations.

#### Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922)

The movement opposed colonial rule through boycott of government institutions, schools, courts, titles, and foreign goods. It drew on the Khilafat movement and wider grievances after the First World War and the 1919 Jallianwala Bagh massacre. Gandhi suspended the movement in 1922 after violence at Chauri Chaura, a decision that produced debate within nationalism.

#### Civil Disobedience Movement and Salt March (1930–1934)

Gandhi’s Salt March from Sabarmati to Dandi in March–April 1930 protested the colonial salt tax and symbolized the right to resist unjust laws. Civil disobedience spread through boycotts, salt-making, refusal of certain taxes, and other actions. Women participated prominently, and the campaign drew international attention. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact of 1931 led to participation in the Second Round Table Conference, but negotiations did not resolve the demand for independence.

#### Quit India Movement (1942)

Launched by the Congress in August 1942 during the Second World War, the Quit India Movement demanded an end to British rule. Congress leaders were quickly arrested. Protests, strikes, sabotage, and local parallel administrations emerged in some places, alongside severe repression. Participation and methods varied; the movement did not immediately force British withdrawal but marked a major escalation of mass opposition.

### Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army

Subhas Chandra Bose, a former Congress president, sought to secure independence through international alliances during the Second World War. He led the Indian National Army (INA), which included Indian prisoners of war and expatriates in Southeast Asia; earlier INA organization was associated with Mohan Singh and later leadership with Bose and the Provisional Government of Free India, declared in 1943. INA campaigns fought alongside Japanese forces in the Burma and northeastern India theater. The military campaign failed, but the INA trials of 1945–1946 and reactions within India intensified political debate and affected perceptions of the armed forces.

### Role of women

Women participated as organizers, marchers, writers, revolutionaries, fundraisers, and political leaders. Examples include Sarojini Naidu, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani, Usha Mehta, Kasturba Gandhi, Madam Bhikaji Cama, and Captain Lakshmi Sahgal of the INA. Their roles differed by class, region, ideology, and access to public life.

### Workers, peasants, tribal communities, and marginalized groups

- **Workers:** Trade unions and labor actions grew in cities and industrial centers. Leaders and organizations differed in their relationship to Congress, socialism, and communism.
- **Peasants:** Agrarian movements addressed rents, debt, land rights, forced cultivation, and revenue demands. The Kisan Sabhas and regional movements were important.
- **Tribal communities:** Adivasi communities resisted colonial forest laws, land alienation, taxation, and local exploitation. The Munda Ulgulan associated with Birsa Munda (1899–1900), the Santhal rebellion (1855–1856), and other movements had distinct contexts and should not be collapsed into a single national program.
- **Dalit and anti-caste movements:** Jyotirao Phule, Savitribai Phule, B. R. Ambedkar, Periyar E. V. Ramasamy, and many others challenged caste oppression and exclusion. Ambedkar fought for political representation, social rights, and constitutional safeguards. Their visions sometimes differed sharply from dominant Congress approaches.
- **Religious and regional minorities:** Political organizations and leaders articulated a range of positions on representation, federalism, minority rights, and independence. These debates were central to the final years of colonial rule.

### Constitutional negotiations and the road to independence

Major developments included the Morley-Minto reforms (1909), Montagu-Chelmsford reforms and Government of India Act (1919), Simon Commission protests, the Round Table Conferences, Government of India Act (1935), provincial elections in 1937, the Cripps Mission (1942), the Cabinet Mission (1946), and negotiations over transfer of power. The Muslim League, under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, increasingly demanded Pakistan; the Congress and other organizations advanced different proposals for a united or partitioned political future.

**Significance:** Independence resulted from decades of organized mass action, constitutional negotiation, revolutionary activity, social movements, global conditions, and changes in British power after the Second World War. No single leader or method alone explains the outcome.

**Related topics:** Partition; Constitution; social reform; labor and peasant movements.

## 16. Independence and Partition

### The final years of British rule

**Period:** 1940–1947.

**Background:** The Second World War weakened Britain financially and politically. The Quit India Movement, INA activities and trials, labor unrest, and the Royal Indian Navy revolt of 1946 contributed to a wider crisis of colonial legitimacy. The naval revolt began in Bombay and spread to other locations, but its political meaning and relationship to independence are interpreted in different ways.

### Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)

The British Cabinet Mission proposed a united Indian Union with a limited central government and groupings of provinces. The Congress and Muslim League interpreted parts of the proposal differently, and agreement collapsed amid political mistrust and disagreements about the future constitution and distribution of power.

### Mountbatten Plan and Indian Independence Act

Lord Louis Mountbatten became the last British viceroy in 1947. His June 3 plan accepted partition as the basis for transfer of power. The Indian Independence Act, passed by the British Parliament in July 1947, created the independent Dominions of India and Pakistan. India became independent on 15 August 1947; Pakistan’s independence is commemorated on 14 August in present-day Pakistan, reflecting its legal and ceremonial chronology.

### Partition and human consequences

Partition divided British India largely along religious-demographic lines in Punjab and Bengal, with boundary decisions made by the Radcliffe commissions. The hurried process produced mass migration, communal violence, abductions, sexual violence, family separation, and major loss of life. Estimates of deaths vary widely; millions were displaced. Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and others experienced violence and displacement in different places. Partition should be studied through both political decisions and the experiences of ordinary people.

### Integration of princely states

At independence, hundreds of princely states had different legal relationships with the British Crown. Most joined India or Pakistan through instruments of accession and negotiations. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, V. P. Menon, and other officials played important roles in integration, though local political movements and rulers also shaped outcomes.

- **Junagadh:** Acceded to Pakistan despite a predominantly Hindu population and geographic complications; subsequent events led to its integration into India after a plebiscite in 1948.
- **Hyderabad:** The Nizam initially sought independence; after negotiations and political conflict, Indian forces entered in September 1948 in an operation called “Police Action” by the Indian government. The period included serious violence that has been studied by historians.
- **Jammu and Kashmir:** The princely state’s ruler signed an Instrument of Accession to India in October 1947 amid an invasion by tribal forces from Pakistan and the ensuing war. The dispute became internationalized and remains historically and politically complex.

**Significance:** Independence ended British colonial rule but began the difficult work of state-building, refugee rehabilitation, constitutional government, and managing territorial disputes.

**Related topics:** Indian Constitution; Pakistan; princely states; wars of 1947–1948.

## 17. India After Independence

### Constitution and republic

**Key dates:** The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. It came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a republic.

**Background:** The Constitution established parliamentary government, universal adult suffrage, a federal structure with a strong Union, an independent judiciary, fundamental rights, directive principles of state policy, and a framework for elected legislatures. The constitutional system has changed through amendments and judicial interpretation.

**Key personalities:** B. R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee; Rajendra Prasad was president of the Constituent Assembly and became India’s first president; Jawaharlal Nehru became the first prime minister. The Constitution emerged from the work of a large assembly and many committees, not one individual alone.

### Democratic institutions and elections

India held its first general election in 1951–1952, using universal adult franchise. The Election Commission, Parliament, state legislatures, courts, and civil services became central institutions. Democracy has experienced crises and unevenness, including the Emergency of 1975–1977, when civil liberties were restricted and opposition leaders were detained. The 1977 election resulted in the first national change of government from Congress to a non-Congress coalition.

### Linguistic reorganization of states

The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 reorganized many state boundaries largely along linguistic lines, following public mobilization and political negotiations. Andhra State was created in 1953 after the death of Potti Sriramulu following a fast demanding a Telugu-speaking state. Later changes included Maharashtra and Gujarat (1960), Haryana (1966), and several northeastern states. Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, and Uttarakhand were created in 2000; Telangana was formed in 2014. State boundaries and names continue to reflect political, linguistic, cultural, and administrative negotiations.

### Economic planning and development

India adopted planned economic development after independence, with the Planning Commission established in 1950 and Five-Year Plans beginning in 1951. Early policy emphasized public-sector industry, infrastructure, agricultural development, and import substitution. Land reforms varied by state and had uneven results. The Green Revolution from the mid-1960s increased wheat and rice production in several regions through high-yielding varieties, irrigation, fertilizers, and policy support, but benefits were uneven and environmental costs later became concerns.

Economic liberalization began in 1991 amid a balance-of-payments crisis. Reforms reduced some industrial controls, expanded trade and investment, and changed the role of the private sector. Growth in information technology, services, pharmaceuticals, manufacturing, and digital infrastructure reshaped the economy, while inequality, employment quality, agricultural distress, and regional disparities remained important issues.

### Major wars and security developments

- **1947–1948 India–Pakistan war:** Followed the accession of Jammu and Kashmir to India and fighting involving Indian and Pakistani forces and other combatants.
- **1962 Sino-Indian War:** A conflict over disputed Himalayan boundaries; India suffered a military defeat. Border disputes remained unresolved.
- **1965 India–Pakistan war:** Fought over Kashmir and other fronts; ended with the Tashkent Declaration in January 1966.
- **1971 war:** India intervened in the conflict in East Pakistan amid a refugee crisis and war. The conflict led to the creation of Bangladesh. The Simla Agreement of 1972 set a bilateral framework for postwar relations.
- **1999 Kargil conflict:** Indian forces fought Pakistani soldiers and infiltrators in the Kargil sector of Jammu and Kashmir.
- **Nuclear tests:** India conducted nuclear tests in 1974 and 1998; Pakistan tested nuclear devices in 1998. These developments shaped regional security and international diplomacy.

### Foreign policy

India’s foreign policy under Nehru emphasized non-alignment, anti-colonial solidarity, and strategic autonomy. India helped establish the Non-Aligned Movement with other leaders, though its actual foreign policy also reflected security and economic interests. Relations with the United States, Soviet Union/Russia, China, neighboring countries, and the wider Global South changed across periods. India played roles in the United Nations, peacekeeping, regional diplomacy, and international economic institutions.

### Social reform and political developments

Post-independence India adopted constitutional protections against untouchability and discrimination and expanded affirmative-action policies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, with later policies for Other Backward Classes. Social movements addressed caste violence, gender equality, labor rights, environmental protection, land, language, and regional autonomy. The Hindu Code legislation of the 1950s changed aspects of Hindu personal law; other communities’ personal laws and reform debates followed different paths.

The Emergency (1975–1977), the Mandal Commission recommendations implemented in 1990, the rise of coalition governments, economic reforms, and the growth of regional parties all shaped contemporary politics.

### Science, technology, and major developments

India built institutions in atomic energy, space science, medicine, agriculture, and engineering. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), founded in 1969, developed satellite and launch capabilities; the 1980s saw early indigenous satellite-launch achievements, and later missions included Chandrayaan lunar missions and the Mars Orbiter Mission (2013 launch). India’s nuclear program, pharmaceutical sector, information technology industry, and digital public infrastructure became significant features of the economy and state.

### Developments through the early twenty-first century

Important developments include rapid urbanization, growth of mobile connectivity and digital services, expansion of higher education, changes in media and political communication, and major public-health and environmental challenges. The 2005 Right to Information Act strengthened access to public records; the 2005 rural employment guarantee law created a large public employment program; the 2009 Right to Education Act established a legal framework for free and compulsory elementary education for children in the specified age group. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) was introduced in 2017, and a major constitutional change in 2019 altered the status of Jammu and Kashmir. These events have differing interpretations and effects and should be examined with current legal and historical context.

**Significance:** Since 1947, India has combined electoral democracy and constitutional institutions with major social, economic, and technological transformations, while continuing to face inequalities, regional disputes, and debates over pluralism and rights.

## 18. Indian Art, Culture, and Heritage

### Architecture

Indian architecture includes Buddhist stupas and monasteries, rock-cut caves, Hindu and Jain temples, mosques, tombs, forts, palaces, colonial-era buildings, and modern architecture. Styles reflect regional materials, patronage, religious practices, and exchange.

- **Buddhist architecture:** Sanchi, Bharhut, Amaravati, Ajanta, and other sites preserve stupas, railings, monasteries, and narrative reliefs from different periods.
- **Rock-cut architecture:** Barabar caves, Ajanta, Ellora, Elephanta, Badami, and Udayagiri show varied religious and artistic traditions.
- **Temple architecture:** Nagara styles are prominent in many northern regions; Dravida forms developed in the south; Vesara is a term used in some scholarship for Deccan combinations, though categories can oversimplify regional variation. Examples include Khajuraho, Konark, Brihadisvara at Thanjavur, and temples at Pattadakal.
- **Indo-Islamic and Mughal architecture:** Qutb Minar, Alai Darwaza, Humayun’s Tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, the Taj Mahal, and the Red Fort reflect changing forms of arches, domes, gardens, ornament, and construction.
- **Regional and colonial architecture:** Rajput forts, Deccan monuments, Sikh gurdwaras, Kerala timber architecture, Bengal terracotta temples, and colonial-era civic buildings demonstrate the subcontinent’s diversity.

### Sculpture and painting

Sculptural traditions include Mauryan polished stone pillars, Bharhut and Sanchi reliefs, Gandharan and Mathura art, Gupta sculpture, Chola bronzes, Pala Buddhist art, Jain sculpture, and regional temple imagery. Painting traditions include Ajanta murals, Mughal and Rajput court painting, Deccani painting, Pahari schools, Mysore and Tanjore painting, Bengal School art, and modern movements. Artistic styles changed through patronage, materials, workshops, trade, and religious use.

### Music and dance

Classical music includes Hindustani and Carnatic traditions, each with internal schools and historical changes. Instruments, ragas, performance settings, and patronage vary. Indian dance traditions include Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam, and Sattriya, alongside many regional, folk, ritual, and community traditions. Modern “classical” categories were shaped partly by twentieth-century revival, institutionalization, and changing performance contexts.

### Literature and languages

Indian literary history includes Vedic Sanskrit, classical Sanskrit poetry and drama, Pali and Prakrit Buddhist and Jain texts, Tamil Sangam literature, Kannada and Telugu works, Persian histories and poetry, Punjabi devotional literature, Bengali, Marathi, Malayalam, Assamese, Kashmiri, Urdu, Hindi, and many other traditions. Literary histories are multilingual and interconnected; no single language represents all of Indian culture.

### Ancient and medieval educational centers

- **Taxila:** An ancient learning center in present-day Pakistan, active over multiple periods. It was not a university in the modern institutional sense.
- **Nalanda:** A major Buddhist monastic and learning center in Bihar, especially prominent from the Gupta and post-Gupta periods into the late first and early second millennium CE.
- **Vikramashila:** A Buddhist monastic center founded under Pala patronage in eastern India.
- **Vallabhi:** A learning center in western India known from literary accounts.
- **Kanchipuram and other temple and monastic centers:** Supported learning in religious studies, philosophy, grammar, literature, and other fields.
- **Madrasas, pathshalas, gurukulas, monasteries, and courtly schools:** Educational arrangements varied by region, religion, class, gender, and period. They should not be reduced to one universal system.

### Archaeological sites and UNESCO World Heritage Sites

Examples of important heritage places include:

- **Sanchi Stupa, Madhya Pradesh:** Buddhist monument complex with major phases from the Mauryan period onward.
- **Ajanta Caves, Maharashtra:** Buddhist caves and paintings from several phases, especially the second century BCE and fifth century CE.
- **Ellora Caves, Maharashtra:** Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain monuments developed over several centuries.
- **Dholavira and other Harappan sites:** Archaeological evidence of Indus urbanism.
- **Hampi, Karnataka:** Remains of the Vijayanagara capital.
- **Khajuraho, Madhya Pradesh:** Chandela-era temples.
- **Konark Sun Temple, Odisha:** Thirteenth-century temple associated with Narasimhadeva I.
- **Brihadisvara Temple, Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu:** Chola temple completed around 1010.
- **Taj Mahal, Agra, Uttar Pradesh:** Mughal mausoleum commissioned by Shah Jahan.
- **Qutb Minar and its monuments, Delhi:** Important Sultanate-era complex.
- **Red Fort, Delhi:** Mughal fort-palace complex associated with Shah Jahan.
- **Jaipur, Rajasthan:** A planned historic city recognized for its urban form and architectural heritage.
- **Santiniketan, West Bengal:** Associated with Rabindranath Tagore’s educational and cultural vision.

UNESCO listings and boundaries can change; consult UNESCO’s official World Heritage List for current status. A place’s cultural importance is not dependent solely on World Heritage designation.

## 20. Important Historical Personalities

This section gives concise, searchable profiles. Dates and biographical details are approximate where historical evidence is limited.

### Ancient and early historic figures

#### Chandragupta Maurya
- **Period:** c. late fourth–early third century BCE.
- **Location:** Magadha and a large part of northern India.
- **Contribution:** Founded the Mauryan Empire and established a powerful imperial state.
- **Significance:** His rule laid the foundation for the expansion and administration associated with Ashoka.
- **Related topics:** Chanakya; Seleucus I; Mauryan Empire.

#### Ashoka
- **Period:** c. 268–232 BCE.
- **Location:** Mauryan Empire.
- **Contribution:** Issued rock and pillar edicts promoting *dhamma*, ethical conduct, and respect among communities; supported Buddhist institutions.
- **Significance:** His inscriptions are among the most important primary sources for ancient South Asian political history.
- **Related topics:** Kalinga War; Buddhism; Brahmi script.

#### Gautama Buddha
- **Period:** Traditionally sixth/fifth century BCE; exact dates disputed.
- **Location:** Northeastern South Asia.
- **Contribution:** Taught the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path.
- **Significance:** Founder figure of Buddhist traditions that spread across Asia.
- **Related topics:** Mahajanapadas; Buddhist councils; Ashoka.

#### Mahavira
- **Period:** Traditionally sixth century BCE; exact dates debated.
- **Location:** Eastern Gangetic region.
- **Contribution:** Central teacher in Jain tradition; emphasized non-violence, restraint, and liberation from karma.
- **Significance:** A key figure in the development of Jain religious and philosophical traditions.
- **Related topics:** Jainism; ahimsa; Tirthankaras.

#### Samudragupta
- **Period:** Fourth century CE.
- **Location:** Gupta Empire.
- **Contribution:** Expanded Gupta influence through campaigns and political relationships recorded in the Prayaga Prashasti.
- **Significance:** A major source for the political geography and royal ideology of the Gupta age.
- **Related topics:** Harisena; Gupta Empire.

#### Aryabhata
- **Period:** 476 CE–at least the early sixth century.
- **Location:** Associated with Kusumapura/Pataliputra in scholarly tradition.
- **Contribution:** Wrote the *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE), contributing to mathematics and astronomy.
- **Significance:** Important in the history of mathematical astronomy.
- **Related topics:** Gupta-period scholarship; place-value notation; trigonometry.

#### Kalidasa
- **Period:** Exact dates uncertain; often associated with the Gupta age.
- **Location:** Sanskrit literary world; precise biography uncertain.
- **Contribution:** Poet and dramatist associated with works such as *Meghaduta* and *Abhijnanashakuntalam*.
- **Significance:** A major figure in classical Sanskrit literature.
- **Related topics:** Sanskrit drama; Gupta-era culture.

### Medieval rulers and thinkers

#### Harsha
- **Period:** c. 606–647 CE.
- **Location:** North India.
- **Contribution:** Consolidated a major northern kingdom and patronized religious and literary activity.
- **Significance:** His reign is documented by inscriptions and accounts such as those of Xuanzang.
- **Related topics:** Pulakeshin II; Kannauj; early medieval India.

#### Rajaraja I
- **Period:** c. 985–1014 CE.
- **Location:** Chola kingdom, Tamil region.
- **Contribution:** Expanded Chola power and commissioned the Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur.
- **Significance:** A key ruler in the development of Chola imperial institutions and architecture.
- **Related topics:** Rajendra I; Chola administration; temple architecture.

#### Rajendra I
- **Period:** c. 1014–1044 CE.
- **Location:** Chola realm and maritime networks.
- **Contribution:** Led campaigns into eastern India and a naval expedition toward Southeast Asian trade centers.
- **Significance:** Illustrates the reach of Chola military and maritime power.
- **Related topics:** Indian Ocean trade; Brihadisvara Temple; Srivijaya.

#### Amir Khusrau
- **Period:** 1253–1325.
- **Location:** Delhi and the Persianate cultural world of northern India.
- **Contribution:** Poet, musician, and writer associated with the Delhi court and Chishti Sufi circles.
- **Significance:** A major figure in Indo-Persian literary history; popular claims about inventing particular musical forms should be treated cautiously.
- **Related topics:** Nizamuddin Auliya; Sufism; Persian literature.

#### Kabir
- **Period:** Usually placed in the fifteenth century, though dates and biography vary.
- **Location:** North India, especially traditions associated with Banaras.
- **Contribution:** Poetry challenged religious pretension and emphasized direct devotion.
- **Significance:** Influenced several devotional traditions; his verses survive in multiple collections and community lineages.
- **Related topics:** Bhakti; Sikh scripture; vernacular poetry.

#### Akbar
- **Period:** 1542–1605; emperor from 1556.
- **Location:** Mughal Empire.
- **Contribution:** Consolidated Mughal rule, developed administrative institutions, pursued alliances, and patronized cultural production.
- **Significance:** A central ruler in debates about empire, governance, and religious accommodation.
- **Related topics:** Mansabdari; Todar Mal; Rajput relations.

#### Shah Jahan
- **Period:** 1592–1666; emperor from 1628 to 1658.
- **Location:** Mughal Empire.

## 21. Frequently Asked Questions

### Who founded the Mauryan Empire?
Chandragupta Maurya established the Mauryan dynasty around 322 BCE after the Nanda period. Chanakya/Kautilya is associated with him in later tradition, but the details of that relationship and the textual history of the *Arthashastra* require caution.

### What was the Indus Valley Civilization?
The Indus Valley or Harappan Civilization was a Bronze Age urban civilization whose mature phase dates roughly to 2600–1900 BCE. Major sites include Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, and Kalibangan. Its script remains undeciphered.

### Why did the Indus Valley Civilization decline?
Urban centers contracted after around 1900 BCE and regional cultures emerged. Changes in river systems, monsoon patterns, ecology, trade, and settlement choices may have contributed. No single cause is proven to explain every region.

### What were the Mahajanapadas?
They were sixteen major political realms described in early Buddhist and Jain sources, especially associated with the sixth century BCE. They included Magadha, Kosala, Vajji, Avanti, and others.

### What is Ashoka’s Dhamma?
Ashoka’s *dhamma* was a public ethical policy emphasizing moral conduct, respect for parents and elders, humane treatment, generosity, and respect among religious communities. It was influenced by his Buddhist commitments but was not simply identical to Buddhist doctrine.

### Why is the Gupta period called a Golden Age?
The label refers to achievements in Sanskrit literature, sculpture, mathematics, and astronomy. It is debated because the surviving record emphasizes elite culture and does not establish uniform prosperity or equality across regions and social groups.

### Who built the Brihadisvara Temple?
The Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur was commissioned by Chola ruler Rajaraja I and completed around 1010 CE.

### Who founded the Delhi Sultanate?
Qutb al-Din Aibak established independent rule in Delhi in 1206 after the fragmentation of Ghurid authority. The Sultanate later comprised the Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, and Lodi dynasties.

### Who founded the Mughal Empire?
Babur founded Mughal rule in northern India after defeating Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526.

### What was the mansabdari system?
It was a Mughal ranking and service system that connected an official’s status to administrative and military obligations. It worked alongside revenue assignments such as jagirs.

### What caused the Revolt of 1857?
The cartridge controversy triggered a broader uprising shaped by sepoy grievances, annexation policies, economic pressures, and fears about cultural and religious interference. Causes and participation differed by region.

### What were the main Gandhian movements?
Important campaigns included Champaran (1917), Kheda (1918), Non-Cooperation (1920–1922), Civil Disobedience and the Salt March (1930 onward), and Quit India (1942). Each had distinct aims, participants, and outcomes.

### When was the Constitution of India adopted?
The Constituent Assembly adopted it on 26 November 1949. It came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a republic.

### How did India integrate princely states?
Most princely states acceded to India or Pakistan through legal instruments and negotiations, with political and administrative integration continuing after 1947. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V. P. Menon played major roles in India’s process, alongside local actors and state rulers.

### Was the Indian National Movement only led by Gandhi?
No. Gandhi was highly influential, but the movement also included constitutional nationalists, revolutionaries, socialists, workers, peasants, tribal movements, anti-caste leaders, women, religious and regional organizations, and many local campaigns.

## 22. Glossary of Important Historical Terms

- **Ahimsa:** Non-violence or non-harming; important in Jain, Buddhist, Hindu, and other ethical traditions.
- **Anekantavada:** Jain philosophical principle that reality has many aspects and cannot be fully captured from one viewpoint.
- **Arthashastra:** Sanskrit treatise on statecraft, economy, administration, and political strategy, traditionally attributed to Kautilya.
- **BCE / CE:** “Before Common Era” and “Common Era,” secular equivalents of BC and AD.
- **Bhakti:** Devotional approaches centered on a deity; forms vary across regions and traditions.
- **Brahmi:** An ancient South Asian script used in many inscriptions, including Ashokan edicts; it is ancestral to numerous later scripts.
- **Chauth:** A levy historically associated with Maratha claims to collect a quarter of revenue in certain territories.
- **Dhamma:** In Ashoka’s inscriptions, a public ethical policy; the term is related to Sanskrit *dharma* but must be interpreted in context.
- **Diwani:** Revenue-collection rights; the East India Company obtained these for Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa in 1765.
- **Dukkha:** A Buddhist term often translated as suffering, unsatisfactoriness, or stress.
- **Gana-sangha:** An ancient political formation governed through an assembly or oligarchic body rather than a conventional hereditary monarchy.
- **Harappan:** Relating to the Indus Valley Civilization, named after Harappa.
- **Jagir:** A revenue assignment granted to an official, especially in Mughal and later contexts; not automatically private land ownership.
- **Jati:** A localized social group, often related to occupation, marriage, and social status; distinct from the broad textual category of *varna*.
- **Jizya:** A tax historically levied on certain non-Muslim subjects under some Islamic states; its rules and application varied.
- **Khalsa:** The initiated Sikh community formally established by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699.
- **Mahajanapada:** One of the major territorial realms described in early Buddhist and Jain sources.
- **Mansab:** A rank in the Mughal service hierarchy.
- **Megalith:** A large stone used in a structure or monument; in South Asian archaeology, often associated with burial and commemorative traditions.
- **Meluhha:** A place-name in Mesopotamian sources commonly associated with the Indus region, though details remain debated.
- **Microlith:** A small stone tool, often used as part of a composite implement.
- **Moksha / Nirvana:** Terms for liberation in different Indian religious traditions; their meanings are not identical across traditions.
- **Prashasti:** A eulogistic inscription praising a ruler or patron, often providing valuable but politically shaped historical evidence.
- **Prakrit:** A broad term for Middle Indo-Aryan languages used in inscriptions and literature; it does not refer to one uniform language.
- **Raja / Rajya:** Terms associated with kingship and a realm or state, with meanings that varied by context.
- **Satyagraha:** Gandhi’s method of resistance grounded in truth, non-violence, and disciplined civil action.
- **Silsila:** A spiritual lineage or order in Sufi traditions.
- **Stupa:** A Buddhist commemorative and devotional structure, often associated with relics and circumambulation.
- **Swaraj:** Self-rule; a central idea in Indian nationalist and political thought.
- **Tirthankara:** In Jainism, a spiritual teacher who establishes a path across the cycle of rebirth.
- **Upanishads:** Texts associated with late Vedic traditions and philosophical reflection on self, reality, knowledge, and liberation.
- **Varna:** A broad fourfold social classification in certain Sanskrit texts; it does not fully describe the complexity of lived social organization.
- **Zamindar:** A local landholder, intermediary, or revenue authority whose rights and duties varied across time and region.
- **Zabt:** A form of agrarian revenue assessment associated with Mughal administration, especially in some northern Indian regions.
